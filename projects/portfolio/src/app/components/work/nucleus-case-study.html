Design System · Created 2017 · Led through 2022 · Still in use

NUcleus

One system, many institutional identities.

NUcleus gave University of Nebraska websites a shared front-end foundation without forcing every department and institute into the same visual expression. Leading product direction, design and front-end development, I built the reusable system first, then partnered with developers to integrate it into the university CMS.

The work later expanded into NUcleus for Apps, carrying the same brand and system thinking into university applications.

01

The Challenge

The University of Nebraska System had introduced a new brand, but its digital implementation was fragmented. Departments, institutes and programs served different audiences and operated with different resources. Their sites were built on separate foundations, and common interface needs were repeatedly solved as one-off work.

At the time, I was the sole designer working across the broader university web ecosystem. No one assigned me to build a design system. I recognized the repeated work, audited the existing interfaces and proposed a shared foundation to my team and leadership.

The goal was not to make every university organization look the same. It was to translate the brand into reusable structures flexible enough to support distinct identities, content and institutional goals.

The system also needed to work inside a new CMS, support accessibility and let distributed marketing teams manage their own sites without requiring the central team to design and build every page.

02

Establishing the Product Direction

The project began with an agency redesign that established the visual direction and delivered two coded pages. I preserved that direction while replacing the delivered front-end implementation with a reusable architecture designed for the full university ecosystem.

I pitched NUcleus as a way to improve consistency and reduce repeated design and development. After receiving approval, I created and maintained a roadmap that combined stakeholder requests with needs emerging from active projects.

The roadmap treated NUcleus as a product rather than a one-time website build. New engagements revealed missing components. Existing sites exposed patterns of drift. Accessibility fixes and technical improvements could return to the shared foundation instead of being solved independently by every team.

Product direction did not mean acting as a formal Product Owner. My role combined system strategy, roadmap stewardship, design direction and hands-on front-end development.

03

System Foundations

I structured NUcleus with ITCSS, using layers to separate foundational settings, elements, reusable objects, components and utilities. SCSS variables carried shared decisions for typography, color, spacing and interface behavior.

The reference implementation used HTML and SCSS. Handlebars made it possible to prototype layouts, component combinations and interactions before CMS integration.

This foundation separated the system from a specific production platform. The CMS determined how content editors configured and assembled components. NUcleus defined the front-end patterns, behavior and presentation those components produced.

The architecture supported a practical division of responsibilities:

  • NUcleus provided reusable interface patterns and reference code.
  • CMS developers translated those patterns into configurable components.
  • Site owners controlled their content and page composition.
  • The central team maintained the shared components, tools, guidance and review.

The result was composable infrastructure rather than a rigid set of centrally controlled templates.

04

From Reference Code to CMS Components

I designed and built the reference components, then partnered with CMS developers as they integrated those patterns into Sitecore.

The reference implementation gave developers a concrete definition of the expected markup, responsive behavior and visual result. Collaboration during integration resolved the differences between a controlled prototype and a component that needed to support many sites, content types and editorial choices.

Marketing teams could assemble and manage their sites using shared modules. They retained control of their content and page composition while the central team maintained the underlying system.

That flexibility came with a tradeoff. Reusable blocks made self-service possible, but compelling pages still required judgment about hierarchy, composition, content and accessibility. Training and ongoing guidance were therefore part of the product model, not cleanup after handoff.

Alignment was maintained through direct stewardship rather than a formal contribution program. I reviewed work, helped teams resolve local issues and improved NUcleus when the same problem appeared across multiple sites.

05

A System That Grew Through Real Work

NUcleus developed through active website engagements.

Whenever possible, new sites were designed around existing components. Reuse reduced the amount of new development required and created capacity for higher-value work.

When an engagement introduced a genuinely reusable need, the resulting component returned to NUcleus. Course cards, for example, were first created for Nebraska Online and then became available to other sites through the shared library.

This created a compounding cycle:

Reuse reduced new development → capacity supported better components → new components expanded the library → later sites benefited

Monthly reviews helped identify drift across participating sites. When the issue was local, I worked directly with that team. When the same issue appeared repeatedly, I changed the shared component, guidance or tooling. Often the response required both.

Quarterly meetings introduced new components, shared guidance and helped site teams use the system effectively.

Governance was not only enforcement. The ways teams struggled with the system became product input for improving it.

06

NUcleus for Apps

The new University of Nebraska brand had not yet been translated into the applications I encountered. Theorem created the opportunity to extend the system beyond websites.

The working Theorem interface was built with Handlebars, HTML and SCSS. Its component patterns became the foundation for NUcleus for Apps, a related implementation for role-based university applications.

The release standard called for each component to include:

  • Reference code
  • Configuration options
  • Accessibility requirements
  • Implementation notes
  • Version history
  • Related components

Theorem, NU Contacts and NU Data Access all shipped using NUcleus for Apps before I left the university in December 2022.

The application system remained implementation-aware without being locked to one framework. Reference patterns documented the structure, behavior and visual expectations that engineering teams could carry into their production stacks.

Theorem’s shipped React interface was implemented by engineering from the working reference experience. NUcleus for Apps provided the shared patterns and documentation supporting that translation.

07

Impact

NUcleus supported nine approved university brand expressions and more than 20 departmental and institutional teams.

Before NUcleus, departments often waited for central-team capacity or hired outside design and development help. The shared system gave marketing teams more tools to operate their own sites while preserving access to guidance and support.

Accessibility requirements and fixes moved into shared components instead of being recreated independently by every site team.

Improvements also propagated across the platform. Work funded or requested by one engagement could strengthen the shared system and become available to every participating team.

Nebraska Online provides the clearest example. I led its migration away from an external vendor and directed the front-end and shared-component work that moved it onto NUcleus. Ending the external arrangement removed recurring hosting and maintenance costs. Components developed through the migration returned to the shared collection.

NUcleus also made more efficient use of the central team’s capacity. Reuse reduced repeated implementation work, leaving more time for new components, difficult content problems and direct support where it added the most value.

08

Built to Outlast Its Original Platform

I left the University of Nebraska in December 2022. In 2025, the university moved from Sitecore to Cascade.

I did not plan, lead or contribute to that migration. The later team carried the NUcleus front end into the new platform.

The system survived a change in CMS and a change in the team responsible for it. That durability came from treating the front end as a reusable product foundation rather than an extension of one publishing platform.

NUcleus remains in use today.

Reflection

Direct stewardship kept NUcleus coherent while it was growing, but it also concentrated review and system knowledge in one person.

Given another pass, I would formalize contribution and review earlier. Shared ownership would let teams extend the system without depending on one steward while preserving the standards that made reuse valuable.

The lasting lesson was not that every interface should look alike. A design system succeeds when it gives different teams enough structure to move independently without forcing each of them to rebuild the foundation.
