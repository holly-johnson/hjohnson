Web Platform Practice · 2017–2022

Nebraska.edu

Many sites, one shared foundation.

Departments and institutes across the University of Nebraska System needed distinct websites without rebuilding the foundation each time. I led a repeatable practice on NUcleus, carrying each engagement from information architecture and UX through front-end development, Sitecore build and client training.

The result was a shared system that let marketing teams run their own sites while improvements propagated across the platform.

Role
Led the work end to end across information architecture, UX and visual design, front-end development, Sitecore builds and client training.
Platform
NUcleus website components implemented in Sitecore.
Outcome
Nine approved university brand expressions across more than 20 website and application implementations. NUcleus remains in use today.
01

A Shared Foundation, Not a Shared Look

Departments and institutes had grown separate sites on separate codebases. Visual conventions, content structures and maintenance practices varied by site, leaving the central team to update each implementation independently.

The challenge was not to make every organization look the same. A research institute, an online high school and a university president’s office had different audiences, content and responsibilities.

They needed a shared foundation strong enough to support those differences without rebuilding common interface patterns for every engagement.

NUcleus supplied the reusable components and front-end architecture. The website practice supplied the process for turning each organization’s goals, content and identity into a site its own marketing staff could operate.

02

A Repeatable Engagement

Each project began with alignment around the organization’s goals, audiences and responsibilities.

The practice followed three phases:

Align

Stakeholders, content owners, designers and developers established the business goals, audience needs, project requirements and creative direction.

This work identified what the site needed to accomplish before anyone began choosing page layouts or components.

Map

The goals and content became an information architecture, page inventory and content hierarchy.

Page maps described what each section needed to communicate or help someone do. Wireframes then translated those needs into an arrangement of available NUcleus components.

Build and enable

I developed missing front-end components, assembled the site in Sitecore and worked with content owners, designers and stakeholders to review the build against the agreed page goals.

Training prepared the marketing staff to manage the site after launch. It covered Sitecore, NUcleus components, page composition, accessibility, content practices and SEO.

The engagement did not end at handoff. I continued providing design consultation, problem solving and system guidance when teams needed support.

03

From Content Intent to Shipped Page

Content came before component selection.

A page map established the purpose, hierarchy and action for each section. That let content owners focus on the message and the needs of their audience without having to design the interface themselves.

I then selected the NUcleus patterns that could carry that intent. Wireframes tested the hierarchy and relationships between components before visual design or CMS assembly.

When the existing system supported the need, the page could move directly into implementation. When the strategy required something NUcleus did not yet provide, I worked with brand owners, designers and engineers to design and develop a reusable solution.

The finished Sitecore build was reviewed against the original goals rather than treated as successful because it matched a mockup.

This connected strategy, content and implementation:

Page goal → content hierarchy → component decision → Sitecore build → client ownership

04

The Compounding Library

New sites were designed around existing components whenever possible.

Reuse reduced the amount of new development required for each engagement. That shortened delivery by avoiding work the system had already solved, not by rushing the research or content strategy.

The capacity gained through reuse made room for higher-value component work.

Nebraska Online, for example, needed course cards that the existing library did not provide. I designed and developed them for that engagement. The finished component then returned to NUcleus and became available to other sites.

The value compounded:

Reuse reduced repeated development → capacity supported new components → new components expanded NUcleus → later sites and existing teams benefited

A project could meet its own needs while also improving what the platform offered everyone else.

05

Self-Service Needs More Than Components

NUcleus gave marketing teams reusable building blocks and control over their page composition. The central team retained responsibility for the components, tools, guidance and shared system.

That balance made self-service possible without forcing teams into fixed templates.

It also created a real tradeoff. Access to flexible components did not automatically give every content owner experience with hierarchy, composition, accessibility or web content.

Training and ongoing guidance were therefore part of the product model. They were not cleanup after launch.

I reviewed participating sites monthly for drift. When an issue was specific to one implementation, I worked directly with that team. When the same issue appeared repeatedly, I improved the shared component, guidance or tooling. Often the response involved both.

Quarterly meetings introduced new components, shared examples and helped teams understand how others across the university were using the same system.

Governance was not only enforcement. The places where teams struggled became input for improving NUcleus.

06

Organizational Impact

As the shared toolset expanded, marketing teams could handle more of their own site work.

The central team spent less time maintaining separate front-end implementations. New components and fixes became available across participating sites instead of requiring a separate project for each organization.

Teams could also learn from one another. An institute could see how another group used the same component for a different audience or message, then adapt that pattern to its own work.

The system reduced reliance on external development across the broader ecosystem.

Nebraska Online provides the clearest example. Moving it from an external vendor onto NUcleus removed recurring hosting and maintenance costs. Components created through that migration returned to the shared library instead of remaining unique to one site.

The strongest outcome was not a collection of websites. It was a repeatable practice that turned each engagement into an opportunity to strengthen the shared platform.

The sites have changed since then, and their current layouts belong to their owners. Together they show that distinct organizations could build recognizable identities on the same component foundation.

08

Built to Outlast the Platform

The original website implementation of NUcleus was integrated into Sitecore.

I left the University of Nebraska in December 2022. In 2025, the university moved to Cascade. I did not plan, lead or contribute to that CMS migration.

The later team carried the NUcleus front end into the new platform.

That distinction matters. NUcleus did not remain useful because every historical page stayed unchanged. It remained useful because the shared front-end system was durable enough to outlast its original CMS and the team that created it.

NUcleus remains in use today.

Relationship to NUcleus

NUcleus is the system these sites were built from.

Reflection

The practice proved that reusable components could support independent site ownership without forcing every organization into the same expression.

It also showed that components alone were not enough. Self-service depended on content strategy, training, examples, accessibility guidance and continuing access to design expertise.

Given another pass, I would invest even more heavily in teaching page composition and web-design fundamentals. Better tools reduced the implementation burden. Better guidance helped people use those tools to communicate clearly.
