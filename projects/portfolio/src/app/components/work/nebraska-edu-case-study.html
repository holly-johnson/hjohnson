Practice · University of Nebraska System

Nebraska.edu

The practice that turned a design system into websites.

A design system only pays off when teams can use it. Across the University of Nebraska System, every department and institute wanted its own website and its own message, while the University needed one recognizable voice. The work that resolved that tension was a repeatable engagement: kickoff, architecture, content maps, wireframes, build, training. Running it as lead designer is what kept the brand intact while the ecosystem grew.

01

One University Voice

The University of Nebraska System spans four campuses and measures itself by the impact it has on its people and its state. Brand recognition serves every part of that. When the web presence fragments, the institution sounds like a collection of offices instead of one university.

Each client arrived with a real and specific need. An institute has research to explain. A program has students to recruit. A conference has dates and a registration deadline. None of those needs are served by a template that flattens them.

So the job was never to make every site look the same. It was to connect separate work into a single University voice while leaving each client room to say its own thing.

Consistency is a property of the system. Distinctiveness is what the client came for.

02

A Repeatable Process

One designer cannot hand-craft a strategy for every department in a university system. What scales instead is a sequence that produces the same quality of thinking each time it runs.

Every engagement followed the same six stages. The client always knew which one they were in, what was expected of them, and what they would get back.

03

Structure First

Information architecture came before anything visual. Sorting content into sections and pages is also where search decisions get made, and both are far cheaper to change in a diagram than in a build.

The map below is the structure for the University's online programs. It records more than hierarchy. Notes on the nodes mark which pages already exist, which are new, and which need content moved into them, so the client can see the size of the writing job before committing to the shape.

04

Content Mapping

Content maps split the work along the line that actually divides it. The client sets the hierarchy and owns the message. The design decides which NUcleus components carry that message and in what order.

Each block on the map names what belongs there and what it has to accomplish. A client reading it can tell what they owe without reading a specification, and without being asked to choose a layout.

Wireframes followed directly from the map, composed out of components the system already had. When the strategy needed something that did not exist yet, that request entered the new component process, was designed and developed against the brand, and afterward belonged to everyone on the platform.

05

Build and Hand Over

The build happened in Sitecore, including asset creation and optimization. Review brought content creators, designers, and stakeholders back to the content map and to the goals the client had written for each page, which kept the conversation on whether the page did its job.

Handover was the part that mattered most. Training covered using NUcleus inside the CMS, general web practice, and search, so the client's own staff could run the site afterward. Design consultation stayed available after launch for brainstorming, problem solving, sketching, and further training.

A client who can maintain their own site does not come back for every edit. That is the difference between a service that scales and one that accumulates dependents.

06

Where It Ran

The process ran across departments, institutes, and programs, each one a separate client with its own audience and its own brief.

Because every site drew from the same collection, work done for one client reached the others. A component built to solve one institute's problem arrived in everyone else's toolkit. Teams could also see how another group had used the same components to carry a different message, and borrow what worked.
