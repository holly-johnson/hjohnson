Internal AI tooling · 2025–2026

Orbit

One place to hold a feature, and a working prototype at the end of it.

Designers at Penlink opened features with no requirements. Training learned what shipped when it shipped. Orbit was the workspace built to close that distance. A mission held everything known about a feature, Claude worked that feature alongside whoever opened it, and the output was a prototype assembled from the published Helios library.

Because the prototype was built from real components, it could not promise behavior the system did not have. The whole design team used it on real features.

01

Features Started Without Context

The mandate arrived in one line. UX needed to work out how to use AI. What came back was not an AI experiment. It was a process fix that happened to need a model.

A standing weekly meeting with another designer was the room where it started. That hour existed to build a working partnership rather than trade status, so it could afford to walk the whole feature process looking for the missing piece. The question that landed: what if Claude could help kick a feature off, gather the requirements and stay a partner on it after that?

The practical questions came immediately. Where would the information live. How would it be shared. The answer looked like an app for tracking features. Then the scope opened up, because the same app fit a list of problems nobody had set out to solve.

Showing the idea around settled what it was. Product managers, engineers, QA, training and technical writing each recognized a different piece of it. That made Orbit a service project rather than a design tool. The designer who started it with me put the point better than I did: AI is not replacing roles, it is connecting them.

02

The Mission Model

A feature opened a mission. The mission was the container and it held the whole feature: specs, questions, answers, concerns, notes, research, prototypes and every live link the prototypes produced. It was also the context Claude read, which is what let the partnership continue instead of restarting each session.

Storage was deliberate. A folder per mission, holding JSON files, structured so state behaved like a database rather than a chat history. By the time I left, the team had reached the conclusion that those files needed to become a real database. That is what a proof of concept is supposed to do. It ran far enough to prove its own requirements.

Answering created the mission and an overview of it. From there a single click asked for a few starting directions drawn from what had been collected. That mattered most on features that arrived thin. Rather than waiting for requirements, a designer could put something concrete in front of a product manager and get a fast reaction to it.

The first version of that interview was too long. It got cut down, because the goal was for people to get in and interact, not to complete a form before finding out whether any of it was useful.

03

Prototypes From the Published Library

The interesting part of Orbit was never that it produced a prototype. It was what the prototype was made of.

Generation pulled from the published Helios package, so a prototype could only do what the system actually supported. That constraint is the whole value. A mockup can promise any behavior. A prototype assembled from shipped components cannot, which means what got reviewed was closer to what engineering would receive.

Anything built to finish an idea that Helios could not supply landed in a scaffolding folder instead, which flagged it as outside the library. That gave me somewhere to look. Either the designer should be using a component that already existed, or a new component had just made its case. Gaps stopped needing to be discovered and started reporting themselves.

04

Who It Was Actually For

A prototype that lives on one laptop helps one person. Sharing was the part that made Orbit worth anything to the people downstream of design, so it was built into the mechanics rather than left as a step someone had to remember.

05

Outcomes & Tradeoffs

Orbit was an internal tool and it stayed one. Four designers and a manager used it, which was the entire design organization. Two of them used it heavily. Real features went through it, and it was still in use when I left.

What it needed next was volume. Enough designers taking enough features all the way through to train the thing and improve it, which is the same problem every internal tool has in its first year.

06

Where It Was Headed

The case for Orbit was never throughput alone, though throughput was the easiest part to argue. A working prototype at handoff means engineering starts with more answers and burns fewer rounds of review before release. Training and technical writing get ahead of a release instead of chasing it.

The rest of the case was transparency. Every one of the six gaps in §01 is really the same gap: information about a feature sat with whoever happened to hold it. A mission made that information a shared object, which is a smaller idea than an AI workspace and a more useful one.

Helios and Orbit were built alongside each other and they answer different questions. Helios made a design decision reusable. Orbit was the attempt to find out how much else could be built directly from a system once that system exists.
