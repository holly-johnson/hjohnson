Research and Product Strategy

Investigative Workflow Research

One investigative model for a growing product suite.

As Penlink acquired products with different specialties, the organization needed a shared understanding of how investigative work moved across them.

This cumulative research program connected analyst workflows, product analytics, support feedback and product knowledge into a model the teams could use to identify overlap, expose gaps and decide how each product should support the broader investigative flow.

01

A Portfolio of Different Investigative Models

The research developed throughout my time at Penlink. It became more urgent as acquisitions brought products with different specialties, terminology and assumptions about investigative work into one portfolio.

Investigators moved between tools, information types and product experiences as their work progressed. They carried context across those boundaries themselves.

The suite could not be evaluated as though every product supported one identical workflow.

PLX was most closely associated with evidence-grade investigation. Tangles and Webloc supported open-source exploration and lead generation. Those activities were related, but they carried different standards for sourcing, preservation, reporting and legal defensibility.

The strategic question was not only where products duplicated one another. It was where their workflows should converge, where they needed to remain different and what information had to survive the transition between them.

02

Research Accumulated Over Time

This was not one neatly bounded research sprint.

Evidence accumulated through interviews, workflow observation, prototype testing, stakeholder workshops, product analytics and support feedback. Different methods contributed at different points as the products and organization changed.

Research around PLX included stronger external customer coverage. The open-source perspective relied more heavily on internal analysts, subject-matter expertise, product work and the feedback available through Tangles and Webloc.

That difference is important. The model was grounded in real evidence, but the depth of external research was not equal across every product.

My role was to consolidate what we were learning about open-source work, bring that perspective into broader Product discussions and carry recurring needs between teams that otherwise focused on different parts of the investigation.

The result was a shared frame for interpreting evidence gathered across many projects rather than a single study presented as complete.

03

Two Related Standards of Work

Open-source lead generation helps an investigator determine where to look next.

Information may come from public sources and reveal connections, activity or questions worth pursuing. It can help establish whether formally obtained information is needed, including whether an investigator should seek a subpoena.

That exploratory information is not automatically legal evidence.

Evidence-grade investigation carries a higher standard. Information that may be used in a legal case needs clearer sourcing, preservation, audit history and reporting.

PLX represented the evidence-grade side of the workflow. Tangles and Webloc represented the open-source, lead-generation side.

The products did not need to become identical. They needed to support a defensible transition from exploration into formal investigative work.

That distinction became one of the most important findings in the research.

06

Defining Product Responsibilities

The model helped teams ask clearer product questions:

  • Which stages of the investigation should this product support?
  • Where should its responsibility end?
  • When should another product take over?
  • What context must pass between them?
  • Which differences reflect real user needs?
  • Which differences are unnecessary product inconsistency?
  • Where does the suite leave part of the investigation unsupported?

This reframed overlap as a product-strategy question rather than a simple duplication problem.

Some similar capabilities should converge. Others needed to remain distinct because lead generation and evidence-grade investigation placed different demands on the product.

The model also exposed gaps where no product adequately supported the transition between those modes of work.

07

From Research to Released Product Work

The clearest released example was an integration that allowed PLX users to search Tangles’ open-source information from within PLX.

The shared research helped establish why that connection mattered and shaped the product direction. Product and engineering teams built the integration.

The release reduced one boundary between lead generation and evidence-grade investigation. It let investigators bring open-source discovery closer to their PLX work without treating the two products as interchangeable.

Many smaller product decisions were also informed by the research and released before I left. The larger effect was strategic alignment: teams from companies with different specialties had a shared model for understanding how their products could work together as a platform.

The research was shaping active product initiatives when I left.

08

The Gap That Remained

The larger reporting trail was still incomplete.

Moving a lead from Tangles or Webloc into PLX required more than transferring a result. The handoff needed enough context, sourcing and accountability for the later evidence-grade work to remain understandable and defensible.

Work was underway in three related directions:

  • Stronger reporting within the lead-generation products
  • More structured handoffs into PLX
  • Deeper integrations across the product suite

The PLX–Tangles search integration was a meaningful released step. It did not fully solve the evidence trail or suite-wide interoperability problem.

The research identified and shaped work around the gap, but the complete reporting path had not been delivered when I left.

Reflection

The shared model gave the teams a way to make decisions beyond the feature in front of them.

It connected daily product work to a larger investigative flow and helped distinguish between unnecessary inconsistency and differences the domain genuinely required.

The next research priority would have been deeper external work around the Tangles and Webloc open-source workflow. That evidence could then strengthen the reporting trail into PLX’s evidence-grade work.

The lasting value was not a diagram by itself. It was a shared way to reason about product boundaries, cross-product handoffs and the responsibilities of a growing investigative platform.
