Product · 2017–2022

Theorem

A learning system rebuilt around how the work is actually done.

The University of Nebraska High School teaches a college-prep curriculum entirely online. Its learning management system was decades old and difficult to use. Theorem replaced it, serving students, teachers, instructional designers, customer service, and administrators from one application. I led the experience strategy and design, and built the front end.

Client
University of Nebraska High School
Role
Experience strategy, product design, front-end development
Users
Five groups, each with a separate portal
Built with
HTML, SCSS, prototypes handed to engineering
Outcome

The patterns built here became NUcleus for Apps.

01

A Decades-Old System

The school ran on MyWayCool, an application that had accumulated decades of features and no coherent experience. Everything the school did passed through it. Instructional designers authored courses in it. Students worked through those courses in it. Teachers graded in it, and reached students about deadlines and news through it. Parents checked progress in it. Administrators managed records in it.

One system carried five different jobs, and served none of them well.

The driver behind the rebuild was straightforward: increase student success. Every decision had to answer to that.

02

Discovery

Discovery began in 2017, with the product owner, stakeholders, engineers, and one designer in the room. The first task was to name who the application actually served.

Surveys went out. Each type of administrative user walked through the existing application while describing their process, which surfaced needs and clarified requirements at the same time. That material fed journey maps and card sorting sessions run with stakeholders, and those sessions decided what phase one would contain.

Moodboards and low-fidelity sketches of the product architecture followed, revised as the picture kept changing.

03

Research

Canvas and Moodle were reviewed alongside the product owner, engineers, and stakeholders, feature by feature, to separate what belonged in the first release from what could wait. Alongside that, research into learning management best practice, online learning styles, and conversations with as many students as possible.

The stakeholder requirements did not sit right. They read like descriptions of the old system rather than descriptions of the business.

Engineers instrumented the paths in the existing application to test that. The data showed almost no activity on several of the paths stakeholders had described as essential. Rather than present that as a contradiction, the next round of client sessions used open-ended questions and direct observation of the actual workflow. The user path was then rebuilt around what the work required.

The requirements described the old software, not the work it was meant to support.

04

Analysis

Personas came out of the research, and each one got a storyboard of its experience. The storyboards made the missing features visible, because a gap in a story is easier to see than a gap in a requirements list.

Those storyboards then became the reference for writing product requirements, organized by user portal rather than by feature. Each group of users got an application shaped around its own work.

05

Design and Build

Early sketches went straight into brainstorming sessions with the engineering team. Working through a broad range of solutions together meant design met development early, which ruled options out fast and surfaced opportunities that neither discipline would have found alone.

Wireframes established the basic structure, key elements, and navigation. Once the team agreed on them, prototyping began on the structure of the application itself. Engineers implemented those prototypes as components in the application stack, and design, experience, and functionality testing followed.

As more designers joined, I moved into creative and art direction alongside the design work.

06

What It Became

The prototypes built for Theorem were the first time the University of Nebraska brand had been applied inside an application. Nothing existed to reuse, so the patterns had to be invented here: a card for a course, a table dense enough for administrative work, a way to move between roles.

Those patterns were general enough to survive their first product. They became NUcleus for Apps, and other University applications were built on them.

The sequence matters more than the artifact. A product problem produced the system, not the other way around. Every component in it had already proven itself in something real before it was offered to anyone else.
