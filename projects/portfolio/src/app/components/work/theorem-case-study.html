Product Design · 2017–2022

Theorem

A learning management system rebuilt around how the work is actually done.

The University of Nebraska High School teaches a college-prep curriculum entirely online. I led the experience strategy and design for Theorem and built its front end. Theorem replaced the school’s decades-old learning management system, serving students, teachers, customer service, administrators and instructional designers from one application.

01

One System, Five Jobs

The school ran on Way Cool, an application that had accumulated decades of features and no coherent experience. Instructional designers authored courses in it. Students worked through those courses in it. Teachers graded in it and sent deadlines and news through it. Parents checked progress in it. Administrators managed records in it.

One system, five jobs, none served well.

The driver behind the rebuild was straightforward: increase student success. Every decision had to answer to that.

02

Naming Who It Actually Served

Discovery began in 2017, with the product owner, stakeholders, engineers and one designer in the room. The first task was to name who the application actually served. Five groups came out of that and each one turned out to need a portal of its own.

Surveys went out. Each type of administrative user walked through the existing application while describing their process, which surfaced needs and clarified requirements at the same time. That material fed journey maps and card sorting sessions run with stakeholders. Those sessions decided what phase one would contain.

Moodboards and low-fidelity sketches of the product architecture followed, revised as the picture kept changing.

03

Testing the Requirements

Canvas and Moodle were reviewed feature by feature with the product owner, engineers and stakeholders, sorting what belonged in the first release of Theorem from what could wait. Running alongside it was research into learning management best practice and online learning styles, plus surveys sent to as many students as we could reach.

The stakeholder requirements did not hold up. They described the features of the old system rather than the work the school needed to do.

Engineers instrumented the paths in the existing application to test that. The data showed almost no activity on several of the paths stakeholders had described as essential. Rather than present that as a contradiction, the next round of client sessions used open-ended questions and direct observation of the actual workflow. The user path was then rebuilt around what the work required.

The requirements described the old software, not the work it was meant to support.

04

From Storyboards to Portals

Personas came out of the research and each one got a storyboard of its experience. The storyboards made the missing features visible.

A gap in a story is easier to see than a gap in a requirements list.

Those storyboards then became the reference for writing product requirements, organized by user portal rather than by feature. A requirement belonged to the group whose work it served and was written against that group’s storyboard, so each one could be checked against a step someone actually took.

Organizing the list this way also made the overlaps explicit. Where two groups touched the same object, a course or a grade, the requirement said which portal owned the action and which one only needed to see it. Each group of users got an application shaped around its own work.

05

Design Met Development Early

Early sketches went straight into brainstorming sessions with the engineering team. Working through a broad range of solutions together meant design met development early, which ruled options out fast and surfaced opportunities that neither discipline would have found alone.

Wireframes established the basic structure, key elements and navigation. Once the team agreed on them, prototyping began on the structure of the application itself. Engineers implemented those prototypes as components in the React application stack. Design, experience and functionality testing followed.

Three designers joined the work as it grew. I art directed the Theorem brand they built, across logo, type and color, alongside the product design.

06

What Survived

The prototypes built for Theorem were the first time the University of Nebraska brand had been applied inside an application. Nothing existed to reuse, so the patterns had to be invented here: a card for a course, a table dense enough for administrative work, a way to move between roles.

Those patterns were general enough to survive their first product. They became NUcleus for Apps. Other University applications were built on them.

The sequence matters more than the artifact. A product problem produced the system, not the other way around. Every component in it had already proven itself in something real before it was offered to anyone else.

Theorem launched in April 2021. It is still the system the school runs on.
