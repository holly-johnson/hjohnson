Helios

Design and engineering, finally speaking the same language.

Helios unified a fragmented suite of investigative products through a shared foundation spanning Figma architecture, Angular components, documentation, governance, and adoption. I was the connector between design and engineering: I architected the system and led its development, translating our design team's decisions into the production Angular components that products shipped. The system itself was shaped through UX review and real product needs.

01. Why the System Was Needed

At Penlink, the product ecosystem was a collection of independent investigative tools, including PLX Desktop, PenPoint, GeoTime, Webloc, Tangles, and TanglesGo. Each carried its own brand, visual language, interaction patterns, and engineering history, much of it inherited through acquisition.

The CEO and CPO set a clear directive: unify the suite under one product brand, then move products onto a shared system as their timelines allowed.

“Every team was, in effect, re-solving the same interface problems independently.”

Analysts regularly moved between products to complete investigations. The lack of consistency increased cognitive load and made workflows harder to learn. Every team was, in effect, re-solving the same interface problems independently.

Four designers were supporting over 150 engineers across distributed teams. A four-person design team could not hold the line on consistency by manually reviewing every screen, for every feature, across every product, without inconsistencies slipping through.

At the same time, development could not pause while the system was built. Helios had to unify the product suite through iterative delivery, giving teams shared components and standards they could adopt while continuing feature work.

02. How It Evolved

The system began as a “glow-up” and redesign of Penlink’s flagship product, PLX Desktop, around the time of the merger. At the time, it was called the Penlink Design System, or PLDS. That redesign became the desktop mode in Figma.

For web, the same design language was softened and rounded to better suit the platform. Desktop and web became distinct Figma modes within one system.

Mobile was always a future goal for Helios and remained a consideration in architectural decisions. After Laura and another designer worked through the mobile expression, I was consulted on adding it as another Figma mode.

PLX Web was already in feature development before Helios v1 was ready. To keep work moving, its developers used AI to create interim Angular components in the Helios library and refined them during design reviews. The components were temporary, but the product requirements they revealed were not. Those requirements informed the official Helios components that replaced them.

My role defined the replacement path: complete the official Helios component, merge it into main, publish the library, then have the product team repoint to the supported version.

Tangles had just completed its upgrade to Angular 21, removing the known blocker to adopting Helios. The first step was verifying RTL support and translation, both critical requirements for the existing product suite. The base structure was in place, but the Tangles front-end developer’s deeper expertise and product requirements would shape that area of Helios.

“Each problem could be solved once, then shared across products.”

This was the shared system working as intended. Tangles could contribute RTL and translation requirements that would benefit PLX Web. In return, new capabilities and improved feature UX developed for PLX Web could become available to Tangles through Helios. Each problem could be solved once, then shared across products.

Desktop followed a different path. It adopted the Helios Figma variables, but translating those decisions into XAML remained manual because the UX team did not own or manage the XAML markup.

03. System Foundations & Tokens

Once desktop and web became modes of the same system, their differences needed to be intentional and reusable. Typography, corner radius, color, spacing, and elevation could not be redefined component by component.

A layered token architecture turned those decisions into shared foundations. Primitive tokens defined the available values, semantic tokens described their meaning, and component tokens applied them consistently.

For web, my role connected both sides of the architecture, from the Figma variable structure and token model through the corresponding Angular implementation. This reduced duplicated decisions between design and code.

04. Components & Patterns

Individual components created consistency at the interface level, but products also shared larger workflow problems. Helios expanded to include reusable patterns that showed how components could work together across common investigative tasks.

PLX Web’s timeline bar was one example. After Joel, the product designer, worked through the design for PLX Web, the plan was to build it in Angular as a reusable Helios component and pattern. That would make the same solution available to Tangles and other web products when future feature work called for it, instead of each team solving the same problem again.

05. Governance & Adoption

The Helios site began as a component dashboard for the initial product audit. As the system grew, the dashboard grew with it. It showed where components already existed across products, what was being built in Helios, and where AI-assisted prototypes had pushed a component beyond its supported bounds.

When a prototype was flagged, the plan was for me to review it with the designer and determine whether the AI had changed the component unintentionally or exposed a valid feature requirement Helios needed to support. Live demos and dedicated documentation would follow as the component lifecycle became more connected.

AI assistance started as an experiment in the system work and earned a durable role as it proved its value. I directed Claude to turn repeated manual processes — scanning, capturing, and organizing — into the recurring workflows below.

Adoption made visible

The initial audit became the dashboard’s first recurring workflow. Working with Claude, I set up a scan across all product repositories. It found every existing instance a Helios component needed to account for as an adoption candidate, along with similar implementations that could be aligned as possible adoptions. The results appeared within each component view.

An adoption candidate was the same element already implemented in a product. Once the Helios version was published, the product could repoint to it. A possible adoption candidate was similar enough to investigate, but needed review to determine whether it could align with Helios or had different requirements.

When a product needed properties Helios did not yet support, those requirements were added through non-breaking extensions so the team could adopt without reworking active features. The compromise moved some complexity into Helios, where it was documented for later system-level cleanup.

The recurring scan made adoption opportunities visible for every component. Helios absorbed short-term complexity so teams could adopt with minimal disruption, while the resulting debt remained visible for deliberate cleanup.

Weekly component review

Component review was already part of how our designers evaluated Figma components. I brought that same review into the Helios dashboard so early critique and refinement could stay within UX, where the team shared the same design language. Each week, designers went deep on principles such as contrast, hierarchy, repetition, alignment, and proximity before resolved decisions moved into implementation.

By having Claude capture the discussion from each recording, everyone could participate fully instead of stopping to take notes. It organized the critique into agreed actions and surfaced guidance that could be reused in the Helios documentation site.

I carried the resolved decisions into the Angular component, then brought it back to the UX team for final review. Keeping critique, implementation, and approval connected reduced translation between the Figma component and its coded behavior.

Orbit: where contributions originated

Much of the feature work that fed Helios happened in Orbit — a separate workspace for AI-assisted prototyping and gathering product requirements. When that work revealed a reusable need, a component could move back into Helios as a contribution grounded in real product requirements.

Products were already consuming components built on the shared base. The next maturity goal was to move possible adoption candidates onto Helios, then measure migration and product impact directly. A system succeeds because products use it and development improves, not because the library is large.

06. Design, Verified in Code

Helios created a two-way bridge between product code and the shared system. Existing implementations revealed the behaviors teams already depended on; the finished Helios component returned those requirements to products as a supported, reusable foundation.

I inspected comparable implementations across the product repositories, then evaluated the coded Helios component against that evidence. The component had to preserve required states and API behavior, meet accessibility and keyboard expectations, support translation and RTL layouts, and remain compatible with existing product workflows.

“Production code became research.”

Production code became research. Figma defined the shared intent, Angular tested whether it could survive real engineering constraints, and implementation in a product proved the system could carry the design all the way through.

07. Outcomes & Tradeoffs

Helios gave a small UX team a shared foundation for supporting a growing product ecosystem. Common interaction problems could be solved once, documented, implemented, and reused instead of being redesigned independently across products.

Infrastructure built

Built Helios from its first Figma foundations and token architecture through a published Angular component library.

Teams enabled

Made incremental adoption easier by preserving required product behavior through additive, non-breaking changes.

Operating model established

Connected repository evidence, recurring UX review, documentation, publishing, and product adoption in one component lifecycle.

What remained unresolved

Making adoption easier sometimes moved compatibility debt into Helios. That was a compromise I accepted deliberately, but it increased the work the system would need later.
