01. The Organizational Problem

Penlink’s investigative products had evolved independently, each with its own visual language, interaction patterns, and engineering history. Analysts moved between those products during the same investigation, but the interfaces did not behave like parts of one suite.

Four designers worked across a product organization of more than 150 engineers. Consistency could not depend on reviewing every screen manually, and product development could not pause while a system was built.

Helios had to unify products incrementally while those products continued shipping.

02. Building the Shared Foundation

Helios began as the Penlink Design System during a redesign of PLX Desktop, then expanded into distinct desktop and web modes within one Figma architecture. Shared decisions could vary intentionally by platform without being redefined component by component.

The system connected structured Figma libraries to a production Angular library. Its architecture covered tokens, components, reusable product patterns, documentation, accessibility behavior, internationalization, and contribution workflows.

A layered token model translated primitive values into semantic meaning and component-level implementation, keeping Figma variables and SCSS aligned.

03. Product Needs Shaped the System

Helios did not develop separately from product work. PLX Web was already in feature development, so interim Angular components kept delivery moving while exposing the states, inputs, and behaviors the supported system needed.

Those requirements informed official Helios components that were completed, merged, versioned, and published. Product teams could then repoint to the supported implementation rather than rework active features.

Early Tangles onboarding after its Angular 21 upgrade surfaced localization and right-to-left requirements. Those constraints strengthened the foundation before wider adoption. Desktop used the same Figma variables, although translating them into XAML remained manual because the UX team did not own that markup.

Feature work also revealed reusable patterns for timelines, filtering, notifications, data visualization, and geographic mapping. Those patterns could return to Helios instead of remaining one-off solutions.

04. Making Adoption Visible

A component dashboard made adoption operational by connecting audits, live demos, playgrounds, documentation, readiness, publishing information, and product usage. Claude handled recurring repository scans, separating direct adoption candidates from similar implementations that needed design and API review.

When a product required behavior Helios did not yet support, non-breaking extensions enabled adoption without disrupting active feature work. The resulting system debt remained visible and prioritized in one place rather than disappearing into individual products.

Review without another meeting

Helios component critique moved into the UX meetings the team already held. Designers evaluated components against shared design principles and the investigative workflow research, then used the resolved decisions to refine both the Figma components and production Angular code.

Recorded discussions allowed Claude to capture agreed actions and reusable documentation while the team stayed present in the critique. Components returned to UX for final approval after implementation, keeping Figma intent and coded behavior connected. Production began matching the mocks closely enough that designers could spend more time on user problems and developers could avoid repeated rework.

05. Outcomes & Tradeoffs

The demonstrated outcome was design-to-production continuity: seven PLX Web developers consumed Helios 1.2.0 to ship a customer-facing product from the same decisions defined in Figma.

The broader leverage was still developing. Helios created a supported path for a four-person design team to carry reusable decisions into a product organization of more than 150 engineers, with Tangles beginning the next onboarding effort.

Shared decisions

Desktop and web work gained one language for tokens, components, behavior, and interaction patterns.

Less repeat work

Recurring UI and accessibility needs became supported infrastructure instead of product-by-product fixes.

A working system

PLX Web proved the release path in production while Tangles onboarding tested what wider adoption required.

Better collaboration

Working prototypes gave product, design, and engineering a shared artifact for building stakeholder support and clarifying product needs. In-house analysts and other users could walk through proposed workflows while the team refined the experience in real time.

Tradeoffs

→Consistency vs. product-specific needs. Shared foundations had to leave room for genuinely different investigative workflows.

→Ideal architecture vs. incremental adoption. Rebuilding every product was not realistic with 150+ engineers actively shipping, so Helios coexisted with legacy UI.

→Coverage vs. quality. The four-person team prioritized high-reuse foundations rather than systematizing every interface.

→Control vs. team autonomy. Governance needed enough structure to preserve coherence without making Helios a bottleneck.

→Automation vs. human judgment. Claude reduced repeated scanning and documentation work, while product logic, accessibility, responsive behavior, and edge cases remained human decisions.
