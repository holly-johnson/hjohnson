Design systems are relationship work

The library matters. But the real system lives in the conversations, decisions, and trust that make shared work possible.

Holly JohnsonAugust 21, 2026

It is tempting to describe a design system as a collection of artifacts: tokens, components, documentation, and code. Those things are visible, countable, and easy to put on a roadmap. They are not, by themselves, the system.

A system begins to work when people trust it enough to use it—and trust each other enough to change it. That makes the work less like maintaining a library and more like tending a set of relationships.

The component is the receipt

A finished component is evidence that a group reached a shared understanding. Before it existed, someone had to surface an inconsistency, make room for competing needs, decide what should be common, and explain why the decision would hold up beyond one screen.

The healthiest systems do not eliminate conversation. They make the right conversations easier to have.

This is why adoption cannot be solved through polish alone. A technically perfect component will sit untouched if a team does not understand its decisions, cannot see their own needs reflected in it, or expects contribution to be painful.

Design for participation

The system team’s job is not to own every answer. It is to create enough clarity that more people can help shape the answer responsibly. That means documenting intent, exposing tradeoffs, giving feedback a predictable path, and treating exceptions as information rather than disobedience.

The work compounds. Every clear decision makes the next one easier. Every respectful collaboration lowers the cost of the next contribution. Over time, the system becomes a shared way of thinking—not just a shared set of parts.

A question I’m carrying

What would change if we measured a design system by the quality of decisions it enables, not only the number of components it ships?
